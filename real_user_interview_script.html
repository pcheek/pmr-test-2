Primary Market Research · Round 2 · Human Subjects

Real Prospect Interview Script

Refined from ten synthetic interviews. Every question below carries what the synthetic round suggested and what only a real person can settle. The synthetic round produced hypotheses. This script exists to falsify them.

Version 2.0 Supersedes research/02_interview_script.md Duration 50 to 60 min Questions 14 (2 added, 4 revised) Assumptions ka-001 to ka-007

What changed, and why

The synthetic round did not mostly confirm the plan. It broke four questions and exposed one assumption that was never written down. Those repairs are in this version.

  1. Q7 was presupposing its own answer. Asking "what has stopped it" assumes something did. Eight of ten subjects had no obstacle at all, only no deadline. The question now asks both halves.
  2. A question on capturable assets was missing entirely. Two subjects independently pointed out that most of their balance sits in an employer plan that cannot be moved. Q10 now measures this directly, because it sets revenue per customer more than price does.
  3. The out-loud percentage conversion in the closing question helps some subjects and harms others. It is now conditional on demonstrated numeracy rather than universal.
  4. Fee grievance appears to decay. One subject ranked fees fourth and explained that he would have ranked them first, angry, two years earlier. Q2 now dates the grievance instead of just recording it.
  5. A household spending threshold governs the sale, not affordability. Q13 asks for that dollar figure outright.

Section 1 · Warm-up

1 question · 6 min
Q1Unchanged

Career, asset layout, and peace of mind

Tell me about your career over the last five years or so, and as you go, walk me through where your money ended up living. Then I want to know: when you think about all of it together, what's your gut feeling?

  • You mentioned [account] almost in passing. What's the story with that one?
  • When you say [their exact word], what's behind that?
  • Which of those accounts do you think about least?

What synthetic research suggested

The question works as written and does real load-bearing work. 9 of 10 subjects volunteered a stranded account here without being asked about one, which set up Q7 in every case. Subjects also split cleanly on whether their gut feeling was about amount, complexity, or not knowing, and the third group turned out to be the most motivated buyers.

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether real people volunteer the stranded account this early, or whether the synthetic subjects were being unnaturally forthcoming because they had no reason to protect the information. Watch for embarrassment. A real person may hide the neglected account precisely because it is the neglected one, which would mean Q7 needs its own setup rather than relying on Q1.

Section 2 · Core questions

12 questions · 40 min · riskiest assumptions first
Q2ka-005ka-003Revised

Cost discovery, and when the grievance formed

Walk me through the last time you found out what something in your financial life was costing you. What were you doing when you found out? And when was that?

  • Was that something you went looking for, or did it find you?
  • What did you do with that information?
  • What do you pay now, across everything? (Ask for the number. Then ask how they know it.)
  • Added: How did you feel about it then, and how do you feel about it now?

Do not say: hidden fees, expense ratio, or "how much are they taking."

What synthetic research suggested

Fee awareness is bimodal, not weak. 4 of 10 produced an exact figure unprompted; 3 of 10 could not name any number at all. The added date probe came from one subject who ranked fees fourth and then explained why:

"If you'd asked me in 2022 I'd have said fees, first, angry."Marcus Webb, 38, Early Adopter

That suggests fee grievance decays after the person fires the expensive thing, which would make recency-of-firing a sharper screening criterion than fee sensitivity itself.

What still needs real-world human validation

The decay curve is the single most testable new hypothesis in this study and it rests on one synthetic subject's self-report about his own past emotional state, which is exactly the kind of claim a synthetic persona is worst at. Test it by recruiting deliberately across time-since-firing: 0 to 6 months, 1 to 2 years, 3 years plus. If grievance really decays, the freshly-departed cohort should rank fees first and the others should not.

Q3ka-003Unchanged

Unranked problem inventory

If you could hand off one part of managing your money to someone else and never think about it again, which part would you pick? And then tell me what you'd pick second.

  • What makes that the first one?
  • What's the part you'd absolutely keep for yourself?
  • Where does [pain named in Q1 or Q2] sit on that list?

Do not offer any menu of candidate pains. The unprompted ranking is the entire finding.

What synthetic research suggested

This is the question that invalidated ka-003, and it did so decisively. Fees failed to reach the top two for 9 of 10 subjects. One put fees "around ninth." Another said they were "not in the top ten." What won instead was tax execution labor and not knowing where things stand. The most precise formulation came from the subject who does 40 hours a year of manual lot tracking:

"Fees aren't work, they're the price of the work. Not the pain, absolutely the veto."Sofia Marchetti, 34, Early Adopter

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether "fees are a veto, not a pain" survives contact with people who have never articulated the distinction. It is a clean, quotable formulation, which is a warning sign: synthetic subjects produce tidy aphorisms where real people produce mess. Listen for whether real subjects can hold both ideas at once, or whether the distinction collapses under a follow-up. If it holds, it reorders the whole value proposition: lead on labor, price on trust.

Q4ka-001Unchanged

Revealed delegation history

Tell me about something in your financial life that happens automatically right now, without you doing anything. How did it come to be set up that way?

  • How do you know it's working?
  • When did you last check?
  • What's something you've deliberately kept manual?

What synthetic research suggested

The "how do you know it's working" probe is the most productive single probe in the script. 7 of 10 subjects could not answer it. Three gave a version of "because the balance goes up." This distinguishes convenience tolerance from actual trust, and convenience tolerance is unlikely to survive a drawdown. Only 2 of 10 ran real verification, and both were Innovators or Early Adopters who verify on a schedule.

What still needs real-world human validation

The synthetic round could not test the thing that matters most here, which is what happens to unverified trust during a real market drop. No synthetic subject has ever lost money in a live account. Ask real subjects what they did in a specific dated drawdown they actually lived through, and compare that to what they say they would do. The gap between the two is the real measure of ka-001.

Q5ka-001Unchanged

The failure boundary

Describe a time a system or a company did something with your money that you didn't expect. What happened next?

  • What did you want to happen instead?
  • Who did you want to talk to?
  • What would have made it acceptable?

What synthetic research suggested

The split this question was designed to find is real and it is roughly even. 4 of 10 wanted the automation to be inspectable (show me the rule that fired). 5 of 10 wanted it to be accountable (give me a named person). These are different products, not different sentiments. One subject wanted the rule rewritten rather than a phone call; another said plainly that a website will not call him in a crash.

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether the inspectable group would actually read the rule. Stated appetite for transparency is famously cheap. The test is not hypothetical: send real prospects an actual methodology document and measure whether they open it, how long they spend, and whether they come back with a substantive question. One synthetic subject said she would read it "this weekend," which is exactly the kind of costless promise real behavior contradicts.

Q6ka-002Unchanged

Recalled transfer episode

Tell me about the last time you moved money between institutions, or decided not to. Take me through it from the beginning.

  • How long did the whole thing take, start to finish?
  • How much of that was you working, and how much was waiting?
  • Who did the work?

Do not say: ACATS, "the transfer process," or anything implying transfers are difficult.

What synthetic research suggested

Separate elapsed time from effort time, because they diverge wildly and only one is a barrier. Reported effort ranged from 11 minutes to 45 minutes. Reported elapsed time ran to 34 days. The subject with the worst experience quantified it precisely, at 9 days out of market and roughly $1,300 of drift, and still completed both transfers. Mechanical friction is real and it is not stopping anyone.

What still needs real-world human validation

Real ACATS failures are messier than any synthetic account of them: partial transfers, unsupported securities, cost basis arriving wrong or not at all, positions liquidated without instruction. Ask specifically whether cost basis survived the move. No synthetic subject raised basis corruption, which is a known real-world failure and would be a genuine switching barrier rather than an imagined one.

Q7ka-002Revised

The stranded account, without the presupposition

Earlier you mentioned [account]. Two questions about it. What, if anything, has stopped it from moving? And separately, what would have to happen to start it moving?

  • How long has it been sitting there?
  • Has anything ever come close to moving it?
  • Who would you expect to do the work?
  • If nothing changes, where is it a year from now?

What synthetic research suggested

ka-002 was invalidated 8 of 10, and the original wording was part of the problem. It asked what had stopped the account from moving, which presupposes an obstacle. Almost no subject had one. The skeptic caught the flaw in the question itself:

"Nothing's stopped it. Nothing's started it. Those are different sentences and yours assumed the first one."Wes Tanaka, 42, Skeptic

Stranded balances sat for 3 years, 6 years, 7 years, and 19 months with no obstacle whatsoever. The binding constraint is the absence of a deadline. That makes trigger-event timing the go-to-market lever and makes onboarding polish close to irrelevant.

What still needs real-world human validation

Deadline absence is the study's strongest signal and also its most self-serving, because it points at a problem that marketing can solve. Verify it against behavior, not recall. The real test is a field one: reach two matched cohorts, one inside 30 days of a vest or rollover and one outside any trigger window, and compare conversion. If timing is the lever, the gap should be large and obvious. If it is not, then friction was the real story and the synthetic round talked you out of the right answer.

Q8ka-004Unchanged

Last April, reconstructed

Take me through last tax season. What did you do, who did you talk to, and what surprised you?

  • What did the whole thing cost you, between fees and time?
  • What did you find out too late to do anything about?
  • What did you promise yourself you'd do differently this year?

What synthetic research suggested

Tax pain is real and mostly out of reach. Of the surprises named, the majority sat outside what portfolio automation touches: RSU under-withholding, commission withholding, AMT on an ISO exercise, real estate depreciation. Only 3 of 10 had a surprise that loss harvesting or lot selection would have prevented. One subject has 96% of assets in tax-deferred accounts and correctly concluded the entire value proposition is irrelevant to him.

What still needs real-world human validation

Get the actual return, or at least the actual 1099-B and the actual tax-deferred-to-taxable split. Self-reported account mix is the weakest data in this study and it determines whether the product has any surface at all. Ask to see the documents in the interview. Every subject who described their own mix from memory should be assumed wrong until a statement confirms it.

Q9ka-004Unchanged

The workaround

Describe how you keep track of your investment holdings for tax purposes. Show me the actual thing if you have it open.

  • Who built that?
  • How many hours a year does it take?
  • What does it not do that you wish it did?
  • Tell me about a time it got something wrong. What did that cost?
  • When was it last updated?

What synthetic research suggested

Workaround sophistication is the best predictor of willingness to pay in the study, and only 3 of 10 had one at all. The two serious workarounds carried 16 to 17 and 40 to 45 hours a year and documented real losses: $1,800 of disallowed deductions in one case, $6,400 permanently destroyed under the wash sale rule in the other. Both subjects priced the service from that number rather than from their balance. The "when was it last updated" probe is new and it earned its place immediately: one subject's spreadsheet was 20 months stale and overstated net worth by $58,000.

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether a bottom-up value calculation survives being asked for money. Both sophisticated subjects converted their workaround into a defensible annual figure, one landing at $1,800 to $2,200 and the other at $350 to $500, and neither has ever had to actually part with it. The only real test is a price that must be paid. Take deposits or a paid pilot before believing any of these numbers.

Q10ka-005New

Capturable assets, account by account

Go through your accounts one at a time and tell me, for each one, whether you could move it somewhere else tomorrow if you decided to. Not whether you would. Whether you could.

  • What about the plan at your current employer?
  • Which of these would you never move regardless?
  • What's in the ones that can move, roughly?
  • Is any of it illiquid, or subject to a trading window?

What synthetic research suggested

This question did not exist in version 1 and it should have been the first one written. Two subjects independently corrected the interviewer's arithmetic mid-session, because an active-employer 401(k) or 403(b) cannot be transferred at all. One had $329K of a $480K balance permanently out of reach. The other had $293K of $500K out of reach.

"Twenty-five basis points of four-eighty assumes you have four-eighty. You can't have four-eighty."Kate Hollis, 45, Late Majority

Any revenue model built on stated investable assets overstates revenue per customer by roughly three times. Price sensitivity is a second-order problem next to this.

What still needs real-world human validation

This is the highest-value question in the script and the easiest to verify without an interview at all. The ratio is a fact about account structures, not an opinion, so do not rely on interviews for it: pull the real distribution from plan data, aggregator benchmarks, or a screener on your own waitlist. Use the interview only to catch the cases self-report misses, which are in-service withdrawal eligibility, employer stock with trading windows, and double-trigger RSUs that are not assets yet.

Q11ka-006Revised

Last signup, and the shape of the refusal

Think of the last financial account you opened. Walk me through how you got from deciding to done. Then tell me about a time you were asked to connect one account to another service, and what you decided.

  • Where did you stop or put it aside?
  • What made you pick it up again?
  • At what point, if any, did you want to talk to a person?
  • If they refuse linking: is it about someone stealing the credentials, about a company holding a picture of your finances, or about something else?

Do not say: aggregation, "link your accounts," or "it only takes a few minutes."

What synthetic research suggested

Refusal comes in four distinct shapes, and version 1 treated it as one. The added probe separates them. (1) Security refusal, after a real fraud incident, and absolute: "You could pay me ninety-six dollars and I'm still not typing my login into your box." (2) Data-ownership refusal, which is ideological and not about theft at all. (3) Liability refusal, where the subject would forward the terms to a conflicted incumbent advisor first, which functions as a decline. (4) Structural inability, where the subject is maximally willing and simply cannot authorize her spouse's credentials. Only the fourth is solvable by product design, and only with a household invitation flow.

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether stated refusal predicts actual refusal. People say they will not link accounts and then link them, and the reverse. The honest test is a live flow with instrumentation: measure real drop-off at the linking screen, by segment, rather than asking. One synthetic subject abandoned a signup over a password field instead of OAuth and another over an unnamed aggregator in a data-sharing clause, which are both fixable details no interview would have surfaced reliably.

Q12ka-007Unchanged

Revealed approval process

Think of the last financial decision in your household over a few thousand dollars. Tell me who was involved and how it actually got decided.

  • Who raised it first?
  • What would have happened if [other person] had said no?
  • What did they want to know that you hadn't thought about?
  • Where does the two of you talking about this actually happen?

What synthetic research suggested

The co-approver exists, and the plan has the wrong model of what they ask. Across every subject with a real second decision maker, the co-approver's question was institutional legitimacy, never fees or performance. It was asked in nearly identical words by three different households: who are they, what happens if they go under, is there a person or a queue. Distinguish a real veto from a nominal one: two subjects had a stated veto that had never once been exercised in nine and fourteen years. A veto never used is not a veto. And in one household the co-approver is the incumbent competitor, a spouse doing the work himself for free, whose question was "what would they do that I'm not doing."

What still needs real-world human validation

Interview the spouse. Every word of this finding is one partner's account of the other partner's reasoning, which is the least reliable testimony in the study. Recruit couples and run them jointly for at least five sessions. Note too that this question only reaches households that have a co-approver: 3 of 10 synthetic subjects were single or fully separate, and for them the gate was different, a peer or community reputation check that no product feature can buy.

Q13ka-007ka-005New

The household approval threshold

Thinking about your own household, what's roughly the dollar amount at which a recurring purchase stops being your own call and becomes a conversation with someone else?

  • Is that different for a one-time cost versus something annual?
  • What was the last thing that crossed that line?
  • What happened to the last thing that crossed it and then didn't get decided?

What synthetic research suggested

Two subjects volunteered a threshold unprompted, near $300 and near $500 a year, and in both cases crossing it changed the process rather than the answer. Affordability was explicitly not the issue:

"Four hundred and ninety-five is a no. And not because it's unaffordable, we make two hundred and fifty-eight thousand dollars a year. It's a no because four hundred and ninety-five dollars a year is a kitchen table conversation and ninety-six dollars is not."Anika Deshmukh, 36, Early Majority

This reframes $96 as a permission structure rather than a price: it stays inside personal discretion and never triggers a second decision maker. It also identifies the real failure mode above the line, which is deferral rather than rejection, and deferral never produces a no you can learn from.

What still needs real-world human validation

Whether the threshold is a real behavioral boundary or a plausible-sounding number people invent when asked. It is suspiciously tidy that both figures landed on round hundreds. Test it with pricing experiments, not questions: run the same offer at $96, $240, and $480 annually and measure conversion and time-to-decision by household type. If the threshold is real, time-to-decision should jump discontinuously at the line, not rise smoothly.

Section 3 · Closing willingness to pay

1 question, 3 parts · 10 min
Q14ka-005Revised

Value benchmark, then structure, then price

(a) Set aside what anything costs today. For the work we've been talking about, what would feel like a fair annual price to you, and how did you arrive at that?

(b) Some people pay a flat annual amount for this, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

(c) Here's what I've been working on: [one plain sentence, no jargon]. Two ways it could be priced. Ninety-six dollars a year, flat. Or twenty-five basis points of what you have with us. React to both.

  • Revised conversion rule: convert the percentage to dollars out loud only if the subject produced a number themselves in Q2. If they could not, ask "what would you want to know to compare those two?" and record whether they ask for the dollar figure unprompted. Use the capturable figure from Q10, never the stated balance.
  • Which of those two would make you less likely to sign up, and why?
  • What's the number where you'd stop and think about it?
  • What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

Do not defend the price, re-explain the value, or raise the 1% incumbent unless they do. If they say no, ask what would have to be different, then stop talking.

What synthetic research suggested

Three findings, in descending order of how much they should change your behavior.

The percentage is rejected on structure, not on amount. Two subjects preferred the flat fee at balances where flat cost them more, which is the signature of a principled objection. One foreclosed the retreat explicitly: "if you told me it was one basis point I'd say the same thing."

$96 reads as low capability to part of the segment. Three subjects treated it as a warning rather than a bargain. "There is no version of ninety-six dollars where anybody is looking at my account." Two preferred the more expensive option on signaling grounds alone.

$96 may also be under-priced for the people who want it most. The two subjects with real workarounds benchmarked $350 to $500 and $1,800 to $2,200 respectively, from the bottom up. Combined with the ~$300 approval threshold, that suggests a window around $200 to $300 flat that version 1 never tested.

What still needs real-world human validation

Everything here, more than anywhere else in the study. Stated willingness to pay is the least reliable output a synthetic interview can produce, and the founder asking the question makes it worse in both directions. Three specific tests: put up a real pricing page and measure clicks at $96, $240, and 25 bps; run the one-sentence description past people who have never spoken to you, since the signaling finding is entirely about how the number reads cold; and take a deposit from anyone who says yes. A synthetic subject's "I would pay" costs them nothing, and the only number worth anything is one somebody actually paid.

Before you run this on real humans

The synthetic round did its job, which was to find the broken questions cheaply. It cannot do the next job. Six things to settle before the first real session.

  • Recruit across time-since-firing an advisor (0 to 6 months, 1 to 2 years, 3 years plus). The fee-grievance decay curve is the study's most actionable hypothesis and it rests on a single self-report.
  • Recruit couples and run at least five sessions jointly. Every co-approver finding is currently one partner's account of the other's reasoning.
  • Get the capturable-assets ratio from data, not interviews. It is a fact about account structures. It sets revenue per customer. Do not leave it to recall.
  • Ask to see documents in the room (the 1099-B, the spreadsheet, the statement). Self-reported account mix is the weakest input in the study and it determines whether there is any tax surface at all.
  • Screen for a trigger event and record its date. Deadline absence explained eight of ten non-conversions. Recruit inside and outside the window deliberately so the comparison is possible.
  • Take deposits. No willingness-to-pay finding in this document should be trusted until someone has parted with money.
