Primary Market Research · Synthetic Round · 2026-09-22

Ten Synthetic Interviews

Seven key assumptions tested against ten synthetic end users spanning Innovators to Skeptics. One assumption survived intact. The most consequential finding is not an assumption score at all, it is an arithmetic error in the revenue model.

Subjects 10 Questions 12 each Assumptions ka-001 to ka-007 Validated 1 of 7 Partial 2 of 7 Invalidated 4 of 7

Read this before any number below

These ten subjects are synthetic. They are a structured reasoning exercise over premises already held, not evidence about the market. They cannot be surprised, they cannot produce a fact nobody put into them, and every willingness-to-pay figure here is inference rather than measurement. Treat each percentage as a priority ranking that tells you what to test first with real people, never as a finding. The source business plan was also unavailable, so the Step 3 and Step 5 premises behind these personas were reconstructed from the study brief and recorded in research/00_source_basis.md.

The segment's biggest unmet need

Advance, plain-language, dollar-denominated notice of what is about to happen to your money, as one page a household can share. Eight of ten asked for some version of this without being prompted, and four reached independently for the same phrase: four sentences. It is not a portfolio management need. It is a comprehension-and-timing need, and it shows up in the transcripts as a request to be told, before the fact and in dollars, what a vest or a transfer or a December sale is going to cost.

The uncomfortable part is who asked for it. Every four-figure price in this study was volunteered by one of the four subjects who declined to buy the product. One offered $2,000 a year, signed in the room, for an October projection of her ISO and AMT exposure with a human name attached. Another offered $500 once for a written plan and explicitly $0 in year two. The willingness to pay is real, it is roughly six times larger than the automated product earns from the same people, and it sits outside what the product currently does.

The runner-up need is household financial visibility, named by three subjects and most acute for the high earner who cannot describe $198,000 of her own household's assets. That need is also not portfolio management, and it is blocked by the same wall: she cannot authorise her spouse's accounts. Both needs point the same direction, which is that this segment's pain is informational before it is economic.

Contradictions and surprises

5 findings that contradict the Step 3 and Step 5 premises

1 · The capturable-assets gap. Every revenue figure in the plan is roughly 3x too high.

Two subjects corrected the interviewer's arithmetic mid-session. An active-employer 401(k) or 403(b) cannot be transferred, so the billable base is not the balance a prospect names. Across the cohort the ratio is 29.3%. This was not a listed assumption and it outranks all seven that were. It is also the easiest to verify without interviews at all, because it is a fact about account structures rather than an opinion.

2 · Nothing is blocking the switch. Nothing is starting it either.

ka-002 was invalidated 9 of 10, and the original question wording was part of the failure: it asked what had stopped the account from moving, which presupposes an obstacle. Stranded balances sat for 19 months, 3 years, 6 years and 7 years with no obstacle whatever. The binding constraint is the absence of a deadline. That makes trigger-event timing the primary go-to-market lever and makes onboarding polish close to irrelevant.

3 · $96 a year reads as low capability, not as a bargain.

Six of ten treated the flat fee as a warning. "There is no version of ninety-six dollars where anybody is looking at my account." Two preferred the more expensive option on signaling grounds alone, and one preferred $96 flat over a percentage that would have cost him $62.50. Meanwhile the two subjects with genuine workarounds benchmarked $350 to $500 and $1,800 to $2,200 from the bottom up. The flat fee is simultaneously too low to signal capability and too low to capture the value it delivers.

4 · A household approval threshold near $300 governs the sale, not affordability.

Crossing it converts a personal decision into a scheduled conversation, and the failure mode above the line is deferral rather than rejection. "It's a no because four hundred and ninety-five dollars a year is a kitchen table conversation and ninety-six dollars is not." Said by a subject whose household earns $258,000. This reframes $96 as a permission structure rather than a price, and it means the pricing question and the decision-unit question are the same question.

5 · Fee grievance appears to decay, and jargon does not always repel.

One subject ranked fees fourth and explained that he would have ranked them first, angry, two years earlier, before he fired his advisor. If that generalises, the addressable moment is narrow and recency-of-firing is a sharper screen than fee sensitivity. Separately, the premise that jargon reads as extraction held for six subjects, inverted for one who heard it as professionalism, and produced a third response nobody planned for: one subject met jargon with self-blame rather than suspicion, and for her a published methodology document was a disqualifier rather than proof.

What actually held up

ka-007 was the one clean validation, with five subjects holding a real, exercised veto and a standing venue for the conversation. The co-approver's question was institutional legitimacy in every single case, never fees and never performance: who are they, what happens if they go under, is there a person or a queue. That is answerable, cheaply, and it is currently unanswered. Note the caveat: the sample was deliberately built with four to five co-approvers, so this result is partly designed in and needs real couples to confirm.

Study limitations

Read this as carefully as the findings

These are not customers and this is not evidence. Ten synthetic personas were constructed from premises reconstructed out of the study brief, then interviewed by a script written against those same premises, which means the study is structurally incapable of surprising itself about anything outside its own inputs. Where a persona was built to break an assumption, it broke it, and that is a property of the design rather than a discovery about the market. Four of the ten capturable-asset figures are estimates rather than stated values. The willingness-to-pay numbers are the weakest output here by a wide margin: no synthetic subject has ever parted with money, felt a drawdown, or had to explain a purchase to a real spouse. What this round genuinely produced is a ranked list of things to test and four repaired interview questions. The capturable-assets ratio should be checked against plan data rather than interviews; the deadline-absence finding should be checked with matched cohorts inside and outside a trigger window; and every price in this document should be checked by taking a deposit.
