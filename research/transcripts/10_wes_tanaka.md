### (b) Structure preference

**Interviewer:** Some people pay a flat annual amount for this kind of thing, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

**Wes:** Happy to, because structure is the only part I actually care about.

Share of assets is out. Not expensive — out. Categorically. Here's why, and it's not complicated. If you charge me a percentage of my balance, then when my balance goes up, you get paid more. The balance went up because I saved money, or because the market did something you had nothing to do with. Your workload didn't change. The same rebalance on a hundred thousand and on a million is the same rebalance — it's the same three trades. So you've built a business where your revenue tracks my savings rate, and you've called that alignment. It isn't alignment, it's a royalty. You've got a royalty on my thrift.

And there's a second thing. If you're paid on assets, you have a permanent interest in me having more assets with you, which means you have an interest in telling me not to pay off a mortgage and not to buy a third building. I'm not saying anyone would lie. I'm saying I'd have hired an advisor whose incentive is structurally opposed to the thing I actually do with money. Why would I do that.

Flat annual is fine as a structure. It's honest. It says: here's a service, here's a price, the price doesn't care how much money I have. I could sign that.

Nothing and do it myself is what I do now, and I'd point out that it's not a fallback. It's a real option that's been working.

**Interviewer:** Between flat annual and one-time, where do you land?

**Wes:** One-time, clearly, but flat annual I could at least hear. Flat annual is a subscription and I'd want to know what I'm getting every year that I didn't get in year one. If the answer is "we keep doing it," fine, maybe. If the answer is "access," no.

**Interviewer:** And the ninety-six flat?

I don't want a partner in my savings account. I have a partner, it's my wife, and she doesn't take a percentage.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

## Post-Interview Assessment
*(interviewer notes, not the subject speaking)*

### Assumption verdicts

**`ka-001` Trust in automation — Partially Validated (mechanism only, not the incentive).**
He has real, verified automation (mortgage drafts, insurance, Stessa rent ACH, reconciled monthly on the 7th) and in Q5 explicitly asks for inspectability over accountability — "show me the rule... I'll accept almost any outcome if I can audit how you got there" — but he separates the automation from its owner and attacks the owner: "specifically distrustful of automation whose owner is paid by his balance." Trust in code, no trust in the incentive behind it.

**`ka-002` Switching friction / ACATS — Invalidated.**
The friction is neither mechanical nor emotional; there is no friction at all. "Nothing's stopped it. Nothing's started it. Those are different sentences and yours assumed the first one." He executed a four-month 1031 exchange competently, so transfer mechanics plainly do not defeat him. Reducing transfer steps moves nothing here.

**`ka-003` Opaque fees and jargon as primary pain — Invalidated as primary, validated as ideology.**
Asked to rank what he'd hand off, he named tenant screening/turnover first and capital-improvement receipts second; fees did not appear on the list at all ("it's not a chore, it's a thing I decline to do"). Jargon triggers a hostile flat repeat ("Twenty-five basis points." — then silence) rather than confusion. The fee grievance is real but it is a *structural* objection, not a felt pain competing for priority.

**`ka-004` Tax optimization value — Invalidated for securities, validated for real estate.**
He runs an extensive, verified tax workaround — 27.5-year schedule, cost-seg components on 5/7/15-year lives, exchange basis carryforward, ~15 hours/year, one caught error that "would have compounded for a decade" — and all of it sits in Schedule E territory the product cannot reach. Zero securities surface: "I've never harvested a securities loss in my life... There's nothing on the investment side."

**`ka-005` Pricing and 25 bps capture — Invalidated.**
He rejects percentage pricing on structure, not amount: "the same idea with a smaller number on it," "a royalty on my thrift," and explicitly forecloses the smaller-percentage retreat — "if you told me it was one basis point I'd say the same thing." He also prefers the flat $96 over the cheaper $62.50 at his balance, the exact signature of a principled rather than economic objection, and he reverse-engineered the pricing table unprompted ("the flat fee isn't a philosophy, it's a minimum").

**`ka-006` Onboarding autonomy — Invalidated, data-ownership variant.**
Hard refusal, and he pre-empted the security framing himself: "I'm not worried about getting hacked. My credit union's security is probably worse than whoever you'd be using. It's not about the login." His objection is that a continuous read creates an asset he has handed over for free, and revocability doesn't cure it because "data is an asset that conveys." He already declines this from Stessa and hand-uploads CSVs monthly to avoid it. Not a flow problem.

**`ka-007` Spousal co-approver — Invalidated for this subject.**
Clean domain split (he owns property/investments, she owns her practice books and her own SEP), a nominal veto never exercised because "I don't bring her things that need a veto," and he removed her from the analysis explicitly: "Don't put her down as a blocker. I'm the blocker." Notably she would lean *in favor* — she is the one who said not everything should be in dirt.

### WTP verdict

**Lands on: would not buy** — neither $96 flat nor 25 bps, and free-DIY is not a fallback for him but a working incumbent he defends with numbers.

**Dollar figure he would actually pay: $500, one time, non-recurring,** for a written plan covering both buildings, basis and depreciation position, a sale/1031 model for ~2029, the 401(k), the idle $25K, and distribution timing. He priced it by analogy to his real estate attorney's $800 exchange-agreement review and stated the year-two number unprompted: "Nothing, in year two." He also volunteered that this is not a viable business — "that's a consulting practice, not a company" — which makes it the most credible number in the interview and the only genuinely actionable finding from this subject.

1. **"Twenty-five basis points instead of one percent is the same idea with a smaller number on it.
