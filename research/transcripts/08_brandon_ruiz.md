**Brandon:** The percentage. The eight-fifty. And not just because it's bigger — if you'd led with the percentage I'd have stopped listening, because that tells me you're in the business of my balance. The ninety-six at least tells me you're in the business of doing a job.

**Interviewer:** What's the number where you'd stop and think about it?

**Brandon:** I don't think there is one, going up. Going down — if it were free I'd still say no, because the problem isn't the price. That's what I'm trying to tell you. You could pay *me* ninety-six dollars and I'm still not typing my login into your box.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

**Brandon:** Okay, if you want the real list. Number one, some way in that doesn't involve me handing over passwords. If my 401(k) provider themselves offered your thing as an option inside their site, that's a different conversation entirely, because then it's them and I already deal with them. Number two, five years of history and somebody I actually know using it. My wife's first question is going to be "how long have they been in business," and if the answer is "two years" we're done at the kitchen table. Number three, tell me what it did versus what I'm already doing, in dollars, over ten years, with the fee taken out. Not a percentage. Dollars. Because if the honest answer is "about the same," then say that, and I'll respect you for it and still not buy.

**Interviewer:** What would you cancel to pay for this?

**Brandon:** Nothing, because there's nothing to cancel. [pause] Look, I don't want to be discouraging. I think what you're describing is probably good for somebody. If I had four old 401(k)s from four jobs and a pile of company stock and a big taxable account, I'd probably want help. My coworker Dave might be your guy — go talk to Dave.

But I've got one job, one fund, twenty-two years, and a daughter starting college in eleven months. My problem is that I owe somebody about twenty-six thousand dollars next August and I'm not sure which account it comes out of. That's my problem. You're not selling that.

**Interviewer:** That's really useful. Anything else?

**Brandon:** Just — you asked me early on what I pay and I said I don't know, and I could tell that was the answer you were hoping for. So let me say the other half of it. I don't know what I pay, and I've also never lost money I couldn't explain, never panicked, never sold, and I'm going to have a paid-off house at forty-eight. Not knowing the number and being harmed by the number are two different things. I'd be careful about assuming the first one means the second one.

## Post-Interview Assessment
*(interviewer notes, not the subject speaking)*

### Assumption verdicts

**`ka-001` Trust in automation — Partially Validated, in a way that does not help.**
He has 22 years of verified, checkable automation (deferral visible on every pay stub, quarterly balances logged in a spreadsheet since 2011) and is one of the few subjects who can actually say how he knows it works — but his trust is in *his employer's plan and a target-date fund*, not in a third party, and he explicitly frames the offering as "a second person doing the job the fund already does." He also requires a person for accountability (Denise at the credit union branch, Rick the tax preparer, the AC company since 1994), which is a support-model requirement layered on top of no felt need.

**`ka-002` Switching friction / ACATS — Invalidated.**
He has never once moved investments between institutions in 22 years, so there is no mechanical friction to reduce; the honest blocker he names is the absence of a deadline — "nothing's stopped it. Nobody's asked me to do anything with it" — plus an in-service withdrawal restriction he never bothered to resolve because nothing forced it.

**`ka-003` Opaque fees and jargon as primary pain — Invalidated.**
Fees are not merely low-ranked, they are absent: "They're not on the list... It's not in the top ten," and when asked to imagine a $200 annual fee he said he'd take it and never think about it again; jargon does trigger his suspicion alarm, but as evidence of a sales pitch rather than as a comprehension pain the product can relieve.

**`ka-004` Tax optimization value — Invalidated, cleanly and correctly.**
$316K of his $340K sits in 401(k)/Roth/529 wrappers and he computed the 96% himself unprompted — "ninety-six percent of my money lives somewhere the tax code doesn't reach until I'm sixty-five" — and there is no workaround to displace because none exists or is needed; his only taxable account is $24K of four stocks he intends to liquidate for tuition.

**`ka-005` Pricing and 25 bps capture — Invalidated on structure and on need.**
He rejected 25 bps ($850 at his balance) on structure before hearing the amount — "the bill grows when the account grows, and the account growing is the whole point" — and called $96 genuinely fair while refusing to buy at any price, including free, which locates the objection entirely outside pricing.

**`ka-006` Onboarding autonomy — Invalidated. Hard security refusal.**
He closed a tab rather than enter 401(k) credentials into a benefits calculator, typed the balance by hand instead, and states the position as a household rule co-signed by his wife after a 2022 card compromise: "That's not a 'convince me' no." Note the one narrow opening: he would consider the service if his own 401(k) provider offered it inside their own site, which is a distribution insight, not an onboarding fix.

**`ka-007` Spousal co-approver — Validated, and the strongest in the sample.**
The veto is real and exercised in both directions (she overrode his AC choice; she killed a 2018 truck purchase; the 2009 timeshare is the founding trauma of the rule), it has a standing venue (first Sunday, kitchen table, spreadsheet), and the co-approver's question is recorded verbatim and is institutional, not economic: "How long have they been around and who do we know that's used them" — price is her *second* question. Her five-year wait-and-see default means this household is unreachable by an early-stage provider regardless of value proposition.

### WTP verdict

**Would not buy.** He lands outside all four pricing options. He calls $96 flat "reasonable" and "a fair price" and still declines, and volunteers that free would not change the answer, because the binding constraints are credential refusal and absence of a problem. **Dollar figure he would actually pay for this product: $0.** Note separately that he stated an unprompted willingness to pay **$400–$500 one-time** for a college cash-flow projection and FAFSA/CSS help, benchmarked to his tax preparer's $340 — that is a real budget, for a different product.

### Top 3 objections, in his own words

1. "I'm not giving a website my 401(k) login. I had a card number stolen two years ago and I'm done with that."
2. "I've been putting money in the same fund since 2003 and it's worked out. What exactly am I fixing?"
3. "The bill grows when the account grows, and the account growing is the whole point. And I didn't do anything different to earn a bigger bill."
