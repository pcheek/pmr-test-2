Two hundred, two-fifty feels like a thing I'd pay without a conversation.

I actually have a line on this, I've said it before. Under a couple hundred a year, I don't want to talk about it. I don't want to evaluate it, I don't want to compare it, I don't want to bring it to the kitchen table. Just take the money.

**Interviewer:** And above that?

**Marcus:** Above that it becomes a decision, and decisions cost me more than the money. That's the thing I'd want you to hear. If it's four hundred dollars, the four hundred dollars isn't the problem. The problem is that four hundred dollars is a thing I have to think about and explain, and thinking about it and explaining it is the scarce resource, not the four hundred.

### (b) Structure

**Interviewer:** Some people pay a flat annual amount for this kind of thing, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

**Marcus:** Flat, easily.

And the reason is specific: flat is a number I can say out loud. If someone asks me what this costs, I say "it's a hundred bucks a year." Done. With a percentage, the answer to "what does it cost" is "well, it depends," and "it depends" is how I ended up not knowing I was paying six thousand dollars.

That's the thing. My problem with the percentage isn't that it's a percentage. It's that a percentage doesn't produce a number. It produces a debit, four times a year, that I never see, and then two years later my wife asks what we pay and I can't answer. I've been that guy. I don't want to be that guy again.

**Interviewer:** And the percentage grows as your balance grows.

**Marcus:** Right, and I know that's supposed to bother me on principle, and it does a bit, but I'll be honest — that's not the part that gets me. The part that gets me is the not-knowing. If a percentage produced a plain email every January that said "you paid us four hundred and twelve dollars last year," I'd be much less annoyed by it than I am. Nobody does that. That's not an accident.

**Interviewer:** And the third one, doing it yourself?

**Marcus:** That's what I'm doing. And it's not free. It's just that the cost doesn't come with an invoice. It cost me sixty thousand dollars at one job and four hundred bucks of unnecessary tax last April and about ten years of not harvesting anything. Doing it myself is the most expensive option I've found so far and it looks free on the statement.

### (c) The candidates

**Interviewer:** Here's what I've been working on: it's an automated service that manages your investment accounts and handles the tax side — picking which shares to sell, harvesting losses, dealing with vests — without you doing anything. Two ways it could be priced. Ninety-six dollars a year, flat. Or twenty-five basis points of what you have with us, which at three hundred and ten thousand is about seven hundred and seventy-five dollars a year.

**Marcus:** *[pause]* Ninety-six is obviously fine.

Ninety-six dollars is less than Monarch and TurboTax combined. That's not a decision, that's a yes. That's under my line by half and I wouldn't even take it to the kitchen table as a cost question.

**Interviewer:** *[pause]*

**Marcus:** My only hesitation on ninety-six — and it's mild — is: what am I not getting. Because six thousand dollars bought me four phone calls, and ninety-six dollars is going to buy me what, exactly? I don't mean that as a gotcha. I mean I'd want to know where the line is. If something weird happens with the tender offer, is there anyone? Or is it ninety-six dollars and I'm on my own with a help center?

But that's a question, not an objection. At ninety-six I'd try it and find out.

**Interviewer:** And the other one?

**Marcus:** Seven seventy-five is an advisor number.

That's my honest reaction. It's not "that's too much for what it does" — seven seventy-five for actually handling my taxes properly might be perfectly reasonable, I don't know, the April thing alone might be worth a few hundred. It's that seven hundred and seventy-five dollars a year is the shape of the thing I already cancelled. It's in the same neighborhood as the guy with the four phone calls. And it's a number that grows, which means in five years it's eleven hundred and I haven't agreed to that.

**Interviewer:** Which of those two would make you less likely to sign up, and why?

**Marcus:** The percentage. By a lot. And not mainly for the reason you'd think.

At ninety-six dollars I sign up tonight, by myself, on my phone, and I mention it to my wife at some point as a thing I did. At seven seventy-five I have to have a conversation. Seven seventy-five is over our talk-about-it threshold — anything recurring over about five hundred, we discuss. So now I have to explain a new company, a new fee, and a percentage structure, to someone whose first question is who are they, at eight-forty-five at night.

And what happens is not that she says no. What happens is she says "okay, look into it and let's talk about it next week," and next week the tender election is due and I'm in a planning cycle at work and we never talk about it. That's how this dies. Not rejection. Deferral.

**Interviewer:** So would you pay the seven seventy-five?

**Marcus:** Me personally, if it were my call alone and I believed it worked? Probably yeah. I'd grumble and do it, because the alternative is me continuing to not do it, and I've now got a decade of evidence about how that goes.

But it's not my call alone, and I'd put the odds of it actually getting done at — I don't know, a third? Whereas ninety-six I'd say is ninety percent, this month.

That gap is not about the six hundred and eighty dollars. It's about which of those requires a meeting.

**Interviewer:** What's the number where you'd stop and think about it?

**Marcus:** Two-fifty is where I start doing math. Five hundred is where I ask my wife first. Anything with a comma in it and I'm not asking anyone, I'm just not doing it.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

**Marcus:** Three things, and one of them is not about me.

One: who holds the money. Custodian, name, our account in our name. Two: what happens to our money if you shut down, in one sentence, and the sentence had better be that nothing happens to it.

Three, and this is the one where I'll admit I'm being a little irrational: a phone number. Somewhere on the site. I am not going to call it. In two years I will not have called it once. But if there's no number at all, then the answer to "what if something weird happens" is nothing, and that's the thing I'd have to explain at the kitchen table.

And then separately, for me and not for her: show me what it actually would have done. Like, take last April. I sold twelve thousand dollars for a furnace and picked the worst shares and owed a few hundred extra. Tell me what your thing would have done differently on that specific night in March. Not a percentage of tax alpha. That night, those shares.

**Interviewer:** What would you cancel to pay for this?

**Marcus:** At ninety-six? Nothing. It's not a swap, it's under the noise. My wife wouldn't see it in the budget.

If it did net worth well I'd probably let Monarch lapse eventually, which is another hundred, so it'd actually be free. But I wouldn't cancel anything to start.

**Interviewer:** Last thing. Say we launched next month and I sent you a link. Walk me through what actually happens.

**Marcus:** Realistically? I open it on my phone, I read it for four minutes, I think "this is the thing I've been meaning to do," and then I put my phone down because a kid needs something.

Then I don't do anything for two weeks. Then the tender offer election is due — that's November seventh, and there's about thirty-eight thousand dollars landing in December that I have no plan for — and that forces the issue, and I come back to it because I've got cash and a deadline at the same time. Which, by the way, is the only reason I'm talking to you at all. Six months ago I don't take this call.

And then I'd screenshot the "who holds your money" page and text it to my wife, and we'd talk about it on a Sunday, and she'd ask her question, and if the screenshot answered it we'd do it.

So — three to five weeks, and it runs through her. Not this conversation. I want to be straight with you about that because I can tell you're hoping I say yes right now. I'm the easy part. I'm not the decision.

**Interviewer:** That's fair. Anything I should have asked and didn't?

**Marcus:** You should ask people what they'd tell their spouse. Not what they think of the product. What sentence they'd use at dinner. Because I'm not the hard sell here, my wife is, and her first question won't be about fees. It'll be "who are they and what happens if they go under." If I can't answer that in two sentences at dinner, this doesn't happen, no matter how good the tax thing is.

Everything else you asked me, I could answer in a way that made me sound like a buyer. That one's the actual gate.

## Post-Interview Assessment

### Assumption verdicts

**`ka-001` Trust in Automation — Partially Validated.**
He has three live automations (401(k) deferral, $1,200/month auto-invest since 2019, two 529 contributions) and explicitly wants the deciding automated ("if something told me here's the seventeen that should be invested, click yes — I'd click yes"), but he cannot verify any of it ("Because the balance goes up. That's the whole verification") and volunteered unprompted that he wants a phone number he will never call. Per the script's own note, that is a support-model requirement rather than a trust failure — but his answer in Q5 was that he wanted *a document*, not a person, which is the inspectable branch, not the accountable branch. The human requirement is proxy demand on his wife's behalf, not his own.

**`ka-002` Switching Friction / ACATS — Invalidated.**
His wife's $41K rollover took two and a half years to start and four months to finish, and he named the binding constraint himself: "Nothing made it two and a half years. That's the honest answer." The friction he described was attentional, not mechanical — a $41K check sitting eleven days under preschool forms — and what unstuck it was Mint shutting down and forcing him to look at all accounts at once. Reducing transfer steps does not reach him; a deadline does.

**`ka-003` Opaque fees and jargon as primary pain — Invalidated.**
Asked to rank, he put taxes first, new-money decisions second, time third, and fees "third, maybe fourth," with the explicit reason that "I already fired the expensive thing. That was the fee problem and I solved it." He also stated the counterfactual cleanly — "if you'd asked me in 2022 I'd have said fees, first, angry" — which suggests fee pain in this segment is an episode with a decay curve, not a standing condition. Jargon does irritate him ("it said something about a transition to enhance the participant experience"), but as an obstacle to speed, not as a signal of extraction.

**`ka-004` Tax Optimization Value — Partially Validated.**
The pain is real, ranked first, and reachable by portfolio automation: $54K taxable plus ~$38K incoming, a FIFO lot-selection error on a $12K sale last year costing an estimated "four to six hundred dollars," and zero deliberate loss harvesting in ten years. But the invalidation condition on the coverage map is triggered — *no workaround exists*. His spreadsheet was last modified January 2024 and overstates his net worth by $58K. He is paying the tax cost in dollars he cannot quantify rather than in hours he can, which means there is no revealed price to anchor against and he had to reach for Monarch and TurboTax to produce a number.

**`ka-005` Pricing and 25 bps capture — Partially Validated.**
$96 flat clears easily and self-serve ("that's not a decision, that's a yes"; his stated threshold is "under a couple hundred a year, I don't want to talk about it"). 25 bps does not capture at his balance: $775 "is an advisor number," and critically his objection to the percentage structure was about *legibility*, not extraction — "flat is a number I can say out loud." He said he would personally probably still pay $775, then immediately put the odds of it actually happening at roughly one in three versus ninety percent for $96, because $775 crosses his household's ~$500 discussion threshold. The $679 difference in price produces a 3x difference in conversion for process reasons, not economic ones.

**`ka-006` Onboarding Autonomy — Validated.**
He linked seven or eight accounts to Monarch himself at 11pm on a phone, never wanted a person ("Never. Zero times. If Monarch had said call us to complete setup I'd have churned that day"), and resolved his one security hesitation by looking up Plaid once and never thinking about it again. Two conditions attach: the read-only answer must appear before he is asked to type anything, and his abandonment point was a 2FA failure that cost nine days of dormancy, so retry and resume matter more than flow length.

**`ka-007` Spousal Co-approver — Validated, strongly.**
This is the cleanest validation in the interview and the most actionable. The approval is real, not consultative — "she has a real no, and I don't push past it" — and he described a veto that operates pre-emptively, in what he declines to propose. The co-approver's question is institutional and he stated it twice, unprompted, in near-identical words: "who are they and what happens if they go under." He also identified the actual failure mode as deferral, not rejection: "What happens is she says okay, look into it and let's talk about it next week, and next week the tender election is due and we never talk about it."
