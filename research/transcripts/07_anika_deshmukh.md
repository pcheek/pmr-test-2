Which means whatever you'd want me to say to him has to fit in about two sentences and cannot require me to defend it, because I can't defend something I don't understand yet. That's the trap. I need the product to make the argument for the product.

## Q12 — Willingness to pay

### (a) Their benchmark

**Interviewer:** Set aside what anything costs today. For the work we've been talking about, what would feel like a fair annual price to you, and how did you arrive at that?

**Anika:** Give me a moment, because I want to arrive at it properly rather than say a number that sounds reasonable.

The closest thing I've ever bought to this is our wills. 2024, after our son was born, a local attorney, fourteen hundred dollars for the two of us — wills, powers of attorney, a guardianship provision. And what I actually remember about it is a forty-minute meeting where a woman explained per stirpes to me on a legal pad, and I left understanding something I hadn't understood when I walked in. That's the only time in my adult life I've paid money and received comprehension. Fourteen hundred, once.

This is smaller than that and it's every year. So — two hundred? Two-fifty a year for a thing that shows me all of it in one place in language I can use, and tells me when something happens before it happens. Two hundred a year I would pay without discussing it with anyone, out of my own account, and consider it cheap. I spend more than that on a meal kit I forget to cancel.

**Interviewer:** How did you land on two hundred rather than five hundred?

**Anika:** Honestly? Two hundred is under the threshold where it becomes a household decision. That's the derivation. It's not a value calculation, it's a permission calculation. Above a certain number I have to bring it to the kitchen table, and above that number the answer is no. So my fair price is partly a function of my marriage, and I'd rather tell you that than pretend I did a discounted cash flow.

### (b) Structure preference

**Interviewer:** Some people pay a flat annual amount for this kind of thing, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

**Anika:** Flat. Strongly, and for a reason I don't think you'll predict.

Everyone's going to tell you the percentage is bad because of incentives. I've heard that argument, it's on the podcast I listen to, and I suppose I agree with it in the abstract. But it's not my reason.

My reason is that a percentage of what we have is a price I am structurally incapable of computing. To know what a percentage costs me, I have to know the balance. Not knowing the balance is the entire problem I am trying to solve. So you'd be selling me a solution to my ignorance at a price that requires me to have already solved my ignorance. I'd be signing up for a fee I could not state, which is precisely the position I'm in now and precisely what I'm trying to get out of.

A flat number is a number. I can put it in a sentence. I can say it to my husband. "It's ninety-six dollars a year" is a sentence I can deliver in the eleven-minute window.

**Interviewer:** And the third — nothing, do it yourself?

**Anika:** That's what we have. That's the incumbent, and the incumbent is not the market. It's a man with a spreadsheet, and it's free, and it works, and it doesn't tell me anything. When you put "do it yourself" on the list you should understand that in my household "yourself" is a different person.

### (c) The actual candidates

**Interviewer:** Here's what I've been working on: it's an automated service that manages your investments and handles the tax side — selling at a loss where that helps, picking which shares to sell, dealing with vesting stock — and shows you everything in one place. Two ways it could be priced. Ninety-six dollars a year, flat. Or twenty-five basis points of what you have with us — at a hundred and ninety-eight thousand dollars, that's four hundred and ninety-five dollars a year. React to both.

**Anika:** Ninety-six dollars is yes. Immediately, personally, yes. That's eight dollars a month, it's less than the meal kit, and I would put it on my own card and not raise it as a topic. And I want to be careful here, because I think you're about to write down a win and it isn't one yet.

Four hundred and ninety-five is a no. And not because it's unaffordable — we make two hundred and fifty-eight thousand dollars a year, four ninety-five is nothing. It's a no because four hundred and ninety-five dollars a year is a kitchen table conversation and ninety-six dollars is not. That's the entire difference between them. The structure isn't the deciding factor, the *approval threshold* is, and you've drawn your two price points on either side of the line that runs through my marriage.

**Interviewer:** Say more about the four ninety-five.

**Anika:** Four ninety-five, I have to say "I want to sign us up for a thing that costs five hundred dollars a year." And the response to that is "what does it do that I'm not doing," and then "it charges us a percentage, so when our balance goes up they get paid more," and then it's over. I lose that conversation. I lose it in ninety seconds and then I don't get to raise it again for a year.

And here's the other thing about your four hundred and ninety-five, and I'd like you to write this down properly. You computed it on a hundred and ninety-eight thousand dollars. Sixty-seven thousand of that is his 401(k). I cannot link his 401(k). I don't have the credentials and I won't ask for them. So either your four ninety-five is a price on assets you can't actually see, or the real number is smaller and so is the product.

If I signed up alone, with only what's mine — my 401(k), my RSUs, maybe the brokerage — you'd have a hundred and twenty-five thousand at most, and you'd show me a dashboard with sixty percent of our money on it and a hole where the rest goes. Which is the app I already deleted.

**Interviewer:** *(five-count silence)*

**Anika:** So the honest version is: I'd pay ninety-six dollars for a partial answer, because a partial answer is more than I have, and because ninety-six dollars doesn't require anyone's agreement but mine. And I'd be a slightly disappointed customer, and I'd probably still renew.

**Interviewer:** Which of those two would make you less likely to sign up, and why?

**Anika:** The twenty-five basis points, without question, and the reason is that it converts me from a buyer into an advocate. At ninety-six dollars I'm a buyer. At four ninety-five I have to go persuade a skeptic, and I'm not equipped for that yet, and the thing I'd be persuading him of is that his work needs supervision.

**Interviewer:** What's the number where you'd stop and think about it?

**Anika:** Three hundred. Somewhere around three hundred it stops being something I just do and starts being something I mention. And the moment I mention it, it's not my decision anymore.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

**Anika:** One page. Made from my accounts only, which is all I can give you, showing me what I have, what it's in, and what it's costing me, in dollars, in sentences. If that page is good enough that I can put my phone on the kitchen island and say "look at this," then you've got both of us. If the page is another grid of abbreviations, you have neither of us and I've wasted ninety-six dollars finding that out, which I can afford.

The demo isn't for me. The demo is ammunition.

**Interviewer:** What would you cancel to pay for this?

**Anika:** Nothing. It's ninety-six dollars. This is the part where I think founders overthink it — there's no trade-off at that price, there's just a threshold. The question was never whether I can afford it. I have no idea what we own and I'm having a second baby in February and going on leave in five months, and the reason I haven't fixed that isn't money.

**Interviewer:** Last thing. Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?

**Anika:** You should have asked what happens if it works.

Because if you show me a real picture of our money, one of two things happens. Either it's fine, and I relax, and I've bought ninety-six dollars of peace of mind. Or it isn't fine — there's something stale, or something's in the wrong place, or the fees are higher than he thinks — and then I'm sitting at the kitchen island with evidence, in a marriage where I've never once brought evidence about money. I've thought about that. I still want it. But your product's actual output, in my house, is a conversation I've been avoiding for eight years, and you should know that's what you're selling me.

I'd still buy it at ninety-six.

## Post-Interview Assessment
*(interviewer notes, not the subject speaking)*

### Assumption verdicts

**`ka-001` Trust in Automation — Partially Validated.**
She would trust an automated system over her husband's spreadsheet *specifically because a system shows her the same view it shows him* ("a system would be wrong in ways we could both see"), but she cannot verify a single piece of existing automation ("the balance goes up — that's my verification method"), and she explicitly wants a human she could talk to ("I would take that appointment, I'd take it on my lunch break"). This is convenience tolerance plus a support-model requirement, not autonomous trust in automation as a substitute for advice.

**`ka-002` Switching Friction / ACATS — Invalidated.**
She has never initiated a transfer in her life; her only rollover cost her "eleven minutes, generously," and she said outright that a product reducing transfer time "would have solved a problem I have never once had." The binding constraint on the stranded account (his $67K 401(k)) is that "nothing has stopped it, because nothing has asked" — no force, no deadline, and no authority on her side.

**`ka-003` Opaque Fees and Jargon as Primary Pain — Partially Validated.**
Opacity is unambiguously her number-one pain, but it is *household* information asymmetry, not provider fee opacity: she ranked consolidation first, taxes second, and fees "fourth or fifth," below college and her leave. She cannot state any fee she pays, refused to estimate on principle, and drew the distinction herself — "I'm not angry about fees. I don't know what the fees are, so how could I be angry about them? I'm angry that I can't find out."

**`ka-004` Tax Optimization Value — Invalidated (as a paid-for benefit for this subject).**
She has no tax workaround of her own; the workaround exists and belongs to her husband (~40-50 hrs/yr, 41 tabs). Her one tax surprise was RSU sell-to-cover under-withholding and a $3,100 April bill — a comprehension and withholding surprise, not a loss-harvesting one. Loss harvesting is already happening in her household for free, invisibly to her: "somewhere in this household there is a person doing tax optimization, and I know about it the way I know about weather in a city I've never visited." She would not pay for tax alpha; she would pay to be told what happened.

**`ka-005` Pricing and 25 bps Capture — Partially Validated ($96 yes, 25 bps no).**
$96 flat is an immediate personal yes; 25 bps / $495 is an immediate no at a household income of $258K, rejected not on affordability and not primarily on incentive grounds but because $495 crosses her spousal approval threshold (~$300) and because a percentage price "requires me to have already solved my ignorance." She also correctly attacked the arithmetic: $495 was computed on $198K including $67K she cannot link.

**`ka-006` Onboarding Autonomy — Invalidated (structurally, not by refusal).**
She is a maximally willing linker — linked Fidelity to Empower in ninety seconds without reading the terms, zero security objection, zero data-ownership objection — and the product still fails, because she cannot authorize her husband's accounts: "I was completely willing. The accounts that would make it worth having are not mine to give." Separately, she abandoned a self-serve 529 signup three times over five weeks and wanted a person at the investment-selection screen but would not call the posted number.

**`ka-007` Spousal Co-Approver — Validated, and in its strongest form.**
The co-approver is not a cautious spouse asking for institutional legitimacy; he is the incumbent competitor, doing the work for free, and his question is "what would they do that I'm not doing, and they'd charge us a percentage for it." Her own veto is theoretical and admitted never-exercised; his is real and has already been used once, in the spring, to close the topic.

### WTP verdict

**$96 flat.** She lands on the flat fee and would actually pay **$96/year**, charged to her own card, without raising it with her husband — and would renew even as a partially served customer with only ~$125K of linkable assets on the dashboard. She would **not** pay 25 bps / $495, and would not pay any figure above roughly **$300/year**, which is where her personal-discretion ceiling ends and spousal co-approval begins. Free-DIY is not an option she is choosing; it is the status quo she is trying to escape, and it is being executed by someone else.

### Top 3 objections (subject's own words)

1. **The co-approver is the competitor.** "He asked what it would do that he isn't already doing. And I didn't have an answer, because the honest answer is 'it would explain it to me,' and I couldn't say that out loud without it landing as 'you don't explain things to me.'"
2. **The percentage is unpriceable by the person who needs the product.** "You'd be selling me a solution to my ignorance at a price that requires me to have already solved my ignorance."
3. **A partial link is the app she already deleted.** "You'd show me a dashboard with sixty percent of our money on it and a hole where the rest goes. Which is the app I already deleted."
