## Q8 — Last April, reconstructed

**Interviewer:** Take me through last tax season. What did you do, who did you talk to, and what surprised you?

Process first. January through March I assemble a package for my CPA: 1099-Bs from Schwab and the plan administrator, W-2s, 1098, the 529 — we don't have a 529, scratch that — the K-1 from a small partnership interest Marco inherited, the charitable stuff, and my own reconciliation spreadsheet of realized gains and losses by lot. In late February she called me. She does not usually call me. She said, "Sofia, I've got a wash sale adjustment on the Schwab 1099 and I think it's bigger than you think it is."

Here's what happened. December 15th, 2025. Seven days after the sale.

**Interviewer:** And the effect was that the loss was deferred.

**Sofia:** No. And I want to stop you there, because this is exactly the thing.

It was not deferred. It was destroyed. Revenue Ruling 2008-5. When the replacement shares are purchased in an IRA, the wash sale disallows the loss and there is no basis adjustment to the replacement shares, because the replacement shares are inside a tax-advantaged account where basis doesn't do anything. In an ordinary wash sale, the disallowed loss gets added to the basis of the replacement shares and you get it back eventually — it's a timing problem. When the replacement is in an IRA, you get nothing. Ever. The loss is gone.

Six thousand four hundred dollars of loss, permanently disallowed. At my combined marginal rate, call it thirty-three point three percent on the ordinary-offset portion and twenty-plus on the rest — the realistic tax value I lost is somewhere between eighteen hundred and twenty-one hundred dollars.

And I'll tell you the part that actually stung. It wasn't the money. It was that I had spent forty hours that year being careful and the failure came from a recurring transfer I'd set up myself, in an account I don't look at, belonging to my spouse, interacting with a ruling I hadn't read. I spent the following weekend reading IRS Publication 550 cover to cover. Which is a genuinely bad weekend, and at the end of it my conclusion was not "I need to be more careful." My conclusion was that this is not a carefulness problem. It's a state-tracking problem across five accounts and two taxpayers and about four hundred lots, and human beings do not do state tracking. That is the entire reason I'm talking to you.

**Interviewer:** What did the whole thing cost you, between fees and time?

**Sofia:** Nine hundred to the CPA. About two thousand in destroyed tax value. And forty hours of my own time across the year, twelve of which were in the second week of December.

I don't bill hourly, so I'll give you the honest framing instead of a fake dollar figure: it's five weekends. Married, no kids, both of us work — we get about a hundred weekends a year that are actually ours. It cost five percent of my free time and it produced a two-thousand-dollar error.

**Interviewer:** What did you find out too late to do anything about?

**Sofia:** The wash sale, obviously — by February it was a done deal, and the settlement-window trick doesn't help you here because there's nothing to re-designate.

The smaller one: I'd have bunched charitable deductions differently if I'd modeled the year properly in November instead of December. **Interviewer:** What did you promise yourself you'd do differently this year?

**Sofia:** I built a household-wide sixty-one-day window tracker in the notebook in March. Every account, both people, every recurring purchase enumerated, with a calendar of blackout dates per security. It took me a weekend and it's the best thing I've built.

## Q9 — The workaround

**Interviewer:** Describe how you keep track of your investment holdings for tax purposes. Show me the actual thing if you have it open.

**Sofia:** I do have it open. *[shares screen]*

Roughly four hundred and ten open lots on the ledger.

On the right, a Jupyter notebook. Schwab doesn't give retail customers an API worth using, so this is CSV-driven. I export the positions and realized-gains files monthly, parse them with pandas, join them onto the lot ledger on an acquisition-date-and-quantity key — which is fragile, because partial sales split lots and Schwab's export doesn't give me a stable lot identifier — and then compute unrealized gain and loss per lot, flag anything below a threshold I set at negative eight percent or three hundred dollars absolute, whichever binds, and cross-reference the flagged securities against the window calendar.

Then it prints a list that says "harvestable today" and "blocked until this date, because of this purchase in this account."

**Interviewer:** Who built that?

**Sofia:** Me. Evenings, over about three months in 2022, and continuously patched since. It's the only code I've written in four years that nobody has reviewed, which is not a comfortable thing for me to say out loud.

**Interviewer:** How many hours a year does it take?

**Sofia:** Maintenance and running it: about two hours a month, so twenty-four. December: twelve in a concentrated block. Ad hoc breakage when Schwab changes a CSV column header, which happened twice: maybe five hours. Call it forty to forty-five hours a year.

**Interviewer:** What does it not do that you wish it did?

**Sofia:** Four things, and they're the spec for whatever you're building, so write them down.

One. It cannot see inside Marco's accounts in real time. I have to manually enumerate his recurring purchases, and the December failure was exactly this. It's not a technical limit, it's that I'm doing it with monthly CSV exports and a human memory.

Two. It cannot see inside the 403(b) or the 401(k) at the holdings level in a way that's useful for wash sales, because those are collective trusts and share classes with no public ticker in some cases. ### (b) Structure preference, without amounts

**Interviewer:** Some people pay a flat annual amount for this kind of thing, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

Flat annual fee is a price for a service. It has the property I care most about, which is that it's a number I can say out loud. "Four hundred dollars a year." Percentage of assets. Here is my position and it is not about affordability.

And let me pre-empt the good version of the counterargument, because there is one and I want to dispose of it honestly. Someone will say: the *benefit* scales with the balance, so the price should too. Value-based pricing. Fine. Does it?

No. And this is the part I don't think the industry has reckoned with. The tax benefit is bounded in ways the balance isn't. Losses offset gains, yes, and that scales. Those two lines diverge for my entire remaining investing life, and they diverge in your favor.

So: flat, then do-it-myself, then percentage. And I want to say clearly that percentage is third even in the cases where percentage would be cheaper, because I'm not optimizing this year's cost. I got a five point seven five percent front load at twenty-two from a man who was very nice to my father, and what I learned from that is that structure is destiny and amounts are marketing.
