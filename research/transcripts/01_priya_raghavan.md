## Q5 — Failure boundary

**Interviewer:** Who did you want to talk to?

**Priya:** *(pause)* Nobody.

## Q12 — Willingness to pay

### (a) Their benchmark

**Interviewer:** Set aside what anything costs today. For the work we've been talking about — the December execution, the lot sequencing, the continuous harvesting — what would feel like a fair annual price to you, and how did you arrive at that?

**Priya:** Let me actually do this rather than guess.

Two ways to arrive at a number. One is my time: sixteen, seventeen hours a year, and the parts I'd actually hand off are maybe nine of those — I'd keep the audit because I like it. Nine hours at a hundred and twenty is about a thousand dollars of my time. But I won't pay a thousand dollars, because I don't value my December Saturdays at my billable rate. Nobody does. Realistically I'd value those nine hours at forty an hour, so three sixty.

The other way is the value delivered. If systematic daily harvesting captures, say, eight thousand dollars of additional losses a year in a normal market versus my once-a-year sweep — and that's generous, but let's say — at my thirty-two percent federal rate with no state tax, that's roughly twenty-five hundred dollars of deferred tax. Deferred, not eliminated, which people always elide. So the honest present value is maybe eight hundred to a thousand, depending on your discount rate and how long you assume the deferral runs. Plus whatever the RSU sequencing is worth, which could be several thousand dollars in one year but is a one-time thing.

So the value is real and it's in the low four figures in a good year. Which means, on value, I could justify five hundred, six hundred dollars a year.

What I'd *actually pay*: two hundred, two fifty. Maybe three hundred if the methodology document is genuinely good.

**Interviewer:** That's meaningfully below what you just said the value was. What's the gap?

**Priya:** *(pause)* The gap is that I can do most of it myself and I know it, and I half enjoy doing it. That's the honest answer.

You're not competing with an advisor. You're competing with my spreadsheet, and my spreadsheet is free and I'm fond of it. So you don't get to charge me the full value of the work — you only get to charge me for the delta between what you do and what my sheet does, discounted by the fact that I might, possibly, this year, finally get around to writing that Python notebook myself. Which I won't. But I believe I will, and that belief has a dollar value and it's working against you.

### (b) Structure preference

**Interviewer:** Some people pay a flat annual amount for this kind of thing, some pay a share of what they have invested, and some pay nothing and do it themselves. Tell me how you think about those three as structures, setting the amounts aside entirely.

**Priya:** Great question and I have a rant prepared, so.

Flat annual fee is the only structure that describes the actual work. The work of harvesting a lot is the same whether the lot is a thousand dollars or a hundred thousand. It's a database query and an order. Your cost to serve me does not scale with my balance. So a flat fee is the honest price of a service, and I'd want to pay it by card, on a date, like a subscription, so I see it leave and I re-evaluate it once a year like an adult.

Percentage of assets is a tax on inertia. And I mean that precisely, not as an insult. The structure's whole economic logic is that your revenue grows without your work growing, and the mechanism by which it grows is *my* savings rate and *the market's* returns, neither of which you contributed to. If I put in twenty-four thousand dollars next year out of my own paycheck, you get sixty more dollars for doing nothing different. Explain the theory under which that's a fee. It isn't a fee, it's a royalty. And the reason it's quoted in basis points is that basis points are a unit designed so the customer doesn't hear a dollar amount, and I've never once encountered a firm that quoted percentages and also volunteered the dollar figure in the same sentence.

Free and do it yourself is where I am, and it isn't actually free, it costs me nine to seventeen hours and a documented error rate. But it's the incumbent and it's a stubborn one, because the cost is denominated in time I've already decided to spend.

**Interviewer:** *(five-second pause)*

**Priya:** ...And the thing about the percentage that I'd add, since you're waiting — it's not that it's expensive. At my balance it's not, it's a thousand dollars, I make two forty-eight, I can obviously afford it. It's that it's a structure I've decided not to participate in, and I made that decision before I met you, and it's not really about you. I've watched people in my family pay one percent for thirty years and it's the single largest wealth transfer in retail finance and nobody involved experiences it as a transaction. I'm not going to be in that pipeline at any denomination.

### (c) The actual candidates

**Interviewer:** Here's what I've been working on: it manages your portfolio automatically and handles the tax side — loss harvesting, which lots to sell, what to do with vesting RSUs. Two ways it could be priced. Ninety-six dollars a year, flat. Or twenty-five basis points of what you have with us, which at your four hundred and twenty thousand is about one thousand and fifty dollars a year. React to both.

**Priya:** *(immediately)* Ninety-six. Obviously ninety-six. That's not even a decision, that's arithmetic. The crossover is thirty-eight thousand four hundred dollars — ninety-six divided by twenty-five basis points — so anyone above thirty-eight grand who picks the percentage has not done the division. I'm at eleven times the crossover.

And your one thousand fifty is already stale, by the way. In six weeks I'll have a hundred and five thousand more in vested stock. If that comes to you, it's thirteen hundred. And my actual goal is financial independence at forty-five, which for me means something like two point six million. Twenty-five basis points on two point six million is sixty-five hundred dollars a year for running the same three funds and the same December query. That's the number your pricing model is actually asking me for, it's just asking for it in fourteen years.

**Interviewer:** Which of those two would make you less likely to sign up, and why?

**Priya:** The basis points, and not for the reason you'd expect.

Here's the part I want you to write down. If you offered me ninety-six dollars flat, or twenty-five basis points at a balance where the basis points came out to *sixty dollars* — cheaper than the flat fee — I would still take the flat fee. I'd pay more to be on a flat fee. I'd pay three hundred flat over a thousand fifty in basis points, obviously, but I'd also pay three hundred flat over two hundred in basis points, and I want you to understand that's not an economic statement, it's a structural one. Because the percentage isn't a price, it's a claim on my future. It's you writing yourself into my compounding. And the day I sign that, every dollar I save has a small piece of you attached to it forever.

So: the basis points don't make me less likely to sign up. They make me not sign up. They're disqualifying, full stop, at any number.

**Interviewer:** And ninety-six dollars flat — what's your reaction to that number specifically?

**Priya:** *(pause)* It's cheap. Suspiciously? No — a little, but not in the way I think you're worried about. Some people will hear ninety-six and assume you can't do much for it. I don't, because I know the marginal cost of the work is near zero once the code exists, and I've written enough software to know that a well-built system doing this for ten thousand people costs almost nothing per person. Ninety-six dollars is a defensible price for a well-engineered thing. It reads to me as "we are not extracting," which is the correct signal.

What ninety-six makes me worry about is your *survival*. Ninety-six dollars a year is eight dollars a month. You need something like fifty thousand paying customers before you're a real company. So my concern with the flat fee isn't that it's too cheap to be good, it's that it's too cheap to persist, and then in three years you either raise it, or you add a percentage tier, or you get acquired by someone who does, or you start monetizing my order flow or my data. Which is the actual thing I'd want to know: what's your business model when the flat fee doesn't cover it. And if the answer involves my data, that's worse than a percentage.

Actually — I'd rather you charged me two hundred and forty. Genuinely. Twenty dollars a month, flat, and then I'd believe it was a business.

**Interviewer:** What's the number where you'd stop and think about it?

**Priya:** Flat? Four hundred is where I'd stop and actually run the value calculation. Six hundred is where I'd probably decline, because at six hundred I'd start thinking I should just write the Python. A thousand flat, I'm out — not on principle, just on value, because at a thousand you're asking me for the full modeled benefit and I'd want to keep some of the surplus.

Percentage? There's no number. Five basis points is a smaller version of the same idea and I'd decline it.

**Interviewer:** What would you need to see before you paid anything at all?

**Priya:** A methodology document, and I mean a real one, and I will read all of it on a Saturday. Specifically, in order:

The lot-selection rules, written as rules. Not "tax-aware" — the actual ordering. Highest basis first? Longest holding period? What's the tie-break? Under what condition does it prefer a short-term loss over a long-term one?

The wash-sale logic, and specifically whether it looks across all my accounts including the 401(k) at my current employer and the stranded one, because that's the thing I can't do and the thing I'm most likely to have gotten wrong.

The replacement-security mapping. If you harvest VTI, what do you buy, and what's your basis for calling it not substantially identical, and has a lawyer looked at that, because the IRS has never defined the term and everyone in your industry is operating on a thirty-year-old convention.

The RSU vest handling. Do you have a sequencing engine for concentration unwind across tax years with a bracket constraint, or do you have a rule that says "sell at vest," because those are extremely different products and the second one is worth about nothing to me.

The security page: OAuth, aggregator by name, credential storage, data use, deletion on cancel.

The ADV Part 2 and the custodian's name.

And an API or at minimum a CSV export, because I am going to keep running my spreadsheet in parallel for at least two quarters and reconcile your output against my own. If I can't export, I can't verify, and if I can't verify I won't stay.

**Interviewer:** What would you cancel to pay for this?

**Priya:** Nothing. Ninety-six dollars a year doesn't displace anything, that's less than I spend on a bad month of DoorDash. I'd pay it out of indifference.

But let me give you the honest version of the whole thing, because I think you're getting a flattering read from me and you shouldn't.

*(pause)*

I'm the easiest yes in your pipeline on price and the hardest yes on conversion. I'll pay ninety-six dollars without blinking. I'd pay two forty. The money is not the obstacle. The obstacle is that I believe I can do this myself — and I'm about seventy percent right about that, which is the dangerous amount of right — and I get something out of doing it. My spreadsheet isn't a workaround I resent, it's a thing I'm a little proud of. You're asking me to admit that a piece of software is more disciplined than I am, which it obviously is, and which I will resist admitting for longer than is rational.

So here's what actually happens if I sign up. I link read-only, after reading the security page. I do not ACATS four hundred thousand dollars to you on day one — that's not happening, I don't care how good the docs are. What I'd do is move the taxable brokerage, the sixty-two thousand, and the stranded old 401(k), the forty-eight, because that one's dead money anyway and moving it costs me nothing emotionally. That's a hundred and ten thousand dollars. I'd run you against my sheet for two quarters, through one December, and I would check your harvest decisions lot by lot against what I would have done. If you found losses I'd have missed, you keep me and eventually you get the RSUs, which is where the real money is. If you did what I do but worse, or if you couldn't explain a single decision I disagreed with, I'd cancel in one sitting, and I'd post about why on r/Bogleheads, and that post would cost you more than my ninety-six dollars was worth.

And the pitch that wouldn't work on me at all is the incentive-alignment one. I know advisors are conflicted. Everybody I read knows. Telling me you're not conflicted is table stakes, it's not a product. I don't need someone to hold my hand and I don't need someone to explain why the other guy is bad. I need something that will actually do the December cleanup I keep telling myself I'll do. And if the price moves when my balance moves, I'm out, because that means I'm paying you for my own savings rate.

**Interviewer:** *(five-second pause)*

**Priya:** ...The one thing I'd add. The twelve hundred shares vest on the sixth of November. If you had a working RSU unwind sequencer I could inspect today, I would pay you more than ninety-six dollars for it, right now, in this call, and I'd move real money. That's a six-week window and then my concentration problem becomes a thing I've lived with for another year and stopped feeling urgent about. So if there's a lesson in me it's probably about timing rather than pricing.
